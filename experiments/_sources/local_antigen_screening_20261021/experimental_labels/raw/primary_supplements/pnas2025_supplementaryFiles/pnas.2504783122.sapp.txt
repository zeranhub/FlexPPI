HERMES neural network model for amino acid propensities19
HERMES is a self-supervised structure-based machine learning model for proteins (1, 2). HERMES has a 3D rotationally20
equivariant architecture and is trained to predict a residue’s amino acid identity from its surrounding atomic environment21
within a 10 Å of the central residue’s C-α in the 3D structure. The input to the neural network is a point cloud of atoms22
from a given structural neighborhood, in which all atoms associated with the central residue are masked. These point clouds23
are featurized by atom types, including computationally-added hydrogens, partial charge, and Solvent Accessible Surface24
Area (SASA). The point clouds are ﬁrst projected onto the orthonormal Zernike Fourier basis, centered at the position of the25
(masked) central residue’s C-α (Fig. 1A). The resultingholographic projections are fed to a stack of SO(3)-equivariant layers,26
the output of which is SO(3)-invariant, and is then passed through a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) to generate the desired27
predictions. The MLP’s outputs are termed pseudo-energies, associated with the 20 diﬀerent possible amino acids that can be28
placed at the masked residue, and act as logits within a softmax function to compute probabilities of each amino acid.29
Each HERMES model is an ensemble of 10 individually-trained architectures. In this work, we used HERMES models30
trained on structural data with diﬀerent levels of added noise: 0.00 noise (the original PDB structure), and 0.50 noise, which is31
obtained by adding Gaussian noise to the 3D coordinates of the original structure, with standard deviation of 0.50 Å. We only32
use models that were trained on the wild-type amino acid classiﬁcation task (1). Models were pre-trained on structures from33
30% similarity split of ProteinNet’s CASP12 set (3), resulting in around∼10k training structures.34
We refer the reader to refs. (1, 2) for further details on the architecture, training procedure, training data preparation, and35
the mathematical introduction to SO(3)-equivariant models in Fourier space.36

To compute peptide energies, HERMES operates on the structural composition of the protein complex. However, data on45
the impact of substitutions on the TCR-pMHC structure is limited, and computational models often fail to capture subtle46
conformational changes in the structure due to amino acid substitutions in a peptide. To address this issue, we adopt two47
protocols to estimate energy for a diverse array of peptides for a given TCR-MHC complex:48
• HERMES-ﬁxed: In the simplest approach, we select the TCR-pMHC structure with matching TCR and MHC to our49
query and with the peptide sequence that most closely matches the peptide of interest and directly input it into HERMES.50
The peptide energy is subsequently calculated as described in eq. 1 (Fig. 1B). This method does not modify the underlying51
structure, eﬀectively assuming that any amino acid substitutions do not signiﬁcantly alter the protein conformation.52
• HERMES-relaxed: Since amino acid substitutions can locally reshape the protein complex, we introduce a more involved53
protocol to account for these structural changes. We begin with the available crystal structure of the TCR-pMHC54
complex that closely matches our query as template. We then mutate the original peptide to the desired state and55
relax the structure using PyRosetta’s cartesian_ddg fastrelax protocol (4). During relaxation, side chain and backbone56
atoms of the peptide are allowed to move, while only the side chain atoms of the MHC and TCR chains within a 10Å57
of the peptide are ﬂexible. We then calculate the peptide energy with HERMES (eq.1), using the relaxed structure58
of the mutated variant as input (Fig. 1B). Since PyRosetta’s relaxation procedure is stochastic in nature, we run 10059
realizations of the relaxation procedure and average the peptide energies across them. For ablation purposes, we also use60
an alternative protocol, whereby we use the HERMES peptide energy from the conformation with the minimum (most61
favorable) Rosetta energy computed on the relaxed atoms; we call this protocolrelaxed-min_energy.62

Peptide design algorithm.Given a TCR and an MHC, we use HERMES to design de novo peptides that can form a stable86
TCR-pMHC complex, and potentially, elicit a T-cell response. Our approach starts with an existing template TCR-pMHC87
structure. We refer to the peptide in the template as wild-type; see Table S5 for the templates we use in our analyses. Similar88
to evaluating the peptide energy, we use two pipelines to sample novel peptides:89
• HERMES-ﬁxed: This is our basic pipeline, in which we generate novel peptide sequences by conditioning on the ﬁxed90
TCR-pMHC template structure. Speciﬁcally, we sequentially mask the atoms of each amino acid along the peptide91
and then use HERMES to compute the probabilityP (σi|xi) of diﬀerent amino acid typesσi at the masked residue92
i, conditioned on the surrounding atomic neighborhoodxi, within a 10 Å radius of the masked residue’s C-α. The93
neighborhood xi can include atoms from the TCR, the MHC, and the other amino acids in the peptide. Repeating this94
procedure for every residue along the peptide allows us to construct a Position Weight Matrix (PWM) that represents the95
probabilities of diﬀerent amino acids at each peptide position, conditioned on the surrounding structural neighborhood.96
We generate peptides using multinomial sampling from the PWM; see the schematic in Fig. 1C.97
• HERMES-relaxed: This pipeline incorporates simulated annealing and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling to98
account for structural changes resulting from peptide substitutions. We start with the template TCR-MHC structure99
and an initial random peptide sequenceσ(0), which isin-silico packed via PyRosetta, followed by the Relax protocol,100
yielding a relaxed structurex(0). During relaxation, both side chain and backbone atoms of the peptide are allowed to101
move, while only the side chain atoms of the MHC and TCR within 10 Å of the peptide are ﬂexible. Following the102
HERMES-ﬁxed protocol, we then evaluate the PWM associated with this relaxed structure and use it to generate a new103
peptide sequence,σ(1). This sequence is again packed and relaxed, producing a new structure,x(1). Using eq. 1, we104
evaluate the energies of the peptides in their respective (relaxed) structures asE(σ(0); x(0)) and E(σ(1); x(1)), and apply105
the Metropolis-Hastings criterion to accept the transition fromσ(0) to σ(1) with probability:106

Filtering metrics for peptide design with HERMES and benchmarking of diﬀerent design approaches.For121
each system, we ﬁrst selected designs predicted by NetMHCPan to be at least weak binders to their target MHCs (EL_rank122
≤ 2.0) (6). We then used TCRdock (7) to ﬁlter peptides for reactivity. TCRdock is a structure prediction algorithm built on top123
of AlphaFold2 (8), and reﬁned to model TCR-pMHC interactions. Its PAE score for the TCR-pMHC interface can be used to124
distinguish reactive from decoy peptides (7), and therefore, can provide a metric for ﬁltering our designed peptides; other work125
has similarly used the PAE of structure-prediction algorithms to ﬁlter designed protein binders (9). Using TCRdock, we retained126
those peptides whose PAE scores at the TCR-pMHC interface fell below a threshold, as illustrated in Figs. S13, S14, and S15).127
The distribution of PAE scores is highly system speciﬁc (9), so we set each system’s threshold near the TCRdock PAE value of128
its wild-type peptides in complex with the respective TCR-MHC, as the wild types are known binders. Among the ﬁltered129
peptides, we selected 93 for experimental validation, ensuring that designs from both HERMES-ﬁxed and HERMES-relaxed, as130
well as from models with 0.0 and 0.5Å noise levels were represented. We also enforced a minimum diﬀerence of 2-3 amino acids131
(depending on the system) between the designs and the wild-type peptides.132
It should be noted that the AF3 PAE score can also discriminate between reactive and decoy peptides (Figs. S8, S16), making133
it also suitable for ﬁltering. However, a full-access implementation of AF3 was unavailable before our experimental validations.134
Therefore, we used AF3 only as a secondary ﬁlter for designs in the EBV and MAGE systems, and for benchmarking against135
TCRdock’s PAE score for the designed peptides (Figs. S8); see details below.136

Fig. S2. Context-dependent mutational effects on T-cell activity. The heatmaps show both experimental measurements and model predictions of T -cell activities of amino
acid variants at positions 1 and 8 of the p53R157H peptide, which we study in complex with the designed bi-speciﬁc antibody H2-scDB and HLA-A*02:01 (21). The wild-type
peptide has a histidine at both sites. A standard substitution matrix (such as BLOSUM62) would assign identical scores to mutations at these two sites, because they share the

with the MHC (from the sides), while H8 is more tightly constrained by polar contacts with the TCR (right panel). HERMES- ﬁxed correctly leverages structural context to predict

accurate in this instance.

Fig. S16.Discrimination ability of diﬀerent scoring schemes as design ﬁlters.The ROC curves show true positive versus false positive rates when
using scores from different models–HERMES, AlphaFold3, and TCRdock (color coded as in the legend)–as scoring criteria for peptide design. Results are shown separately
for designs generated using the HERMES-ﬁxed (left) and the HERMES-relaxed (right) protocols, for the(A) NY -ESO,(B) EBV, and(C) MAGE systems. Experimentally
measured peptide-induced T -cell activity (GFP%) is used as the ground truth for evaluating the predictive performance in each case. For the NY -ESO system only (A) we show
ROC curves computed on both peptides that were predicted to activate T -cells (positives) based on their TCRdock PAE scores (full lines), and the mixture of positive and
negative peptides (dotted lines). For EBV and MAGE (B-C), the scoring criterion is only tested for the peptides that we deemed to be activating (positive) based on their
TCRdock PAE scores (full lines), as these were the ones that we tested experimentally. The HERMES model names specify the design scheme (ﬁxed, relaxed), and the noise
amplitude (in Å) injected to the coordinates of the structure data during training.

Fig. S17. Predicting MHC-I peptide presentation preferences with HERMES. We evaluated HERMES’s ability to recover MHC class I peptide presentation preferences by
analyzing pMHC structures from the TCR3d database (25), grouped by MHC allele and peptide length (L), retaining only those combinations with at least two structures. As
ground truth, we used position weight matrices (PWMs) from the MHC Motif Atlas (11), derived from large datasets of peptide sequences known to be presented by each
allele. HERMES-ﬁxed was used to design peptides for each structure in a given allele-length group, and the resulting PWMs were averaged across structures to generate a
motif reﬂecting the peptide preferences for that MHC I allele (for a given length). As the baseline, we used PWMs generated directly from the peptides found in the structural
dataset. PWM quality was evaluated using the Kullback-Leibler divergence, and the entropy difference from the ground-truth PWM.(A) Shown are the summaries of data and
performances across all MHC I allele-length combinations: number of available structures (ﬁrst row), KL-divergence between the HERMES-derived PWMs (or the baseline) to
the ground truth (second row), the entropy difference to the ground truth (third row), and the entropy of the PWMs based on the three approaches (fourth row).(B) Shown is
the KL-divergence of HERMES-derived PWMs versus the number of available pMHC structures for across different allele-length classes.(C) Examples of pMHC structures
and the corresponding PWMs are shown for four allele-length combinations, spanning a range of structure counts. In each case, structures are aligned along the MHC chain to
show the diversity of peptide poses captured by the ensemble of available structures, used as design templates.
